Where the $4.65 billion comes from, and where it goes

Hospitals that serve many low-income patients can join the federal 340B program and buy outpatient drugs at deep discounts. Medicare has paid them for those drugs as if they paid full price: the drug’s average sales price (ASP) plus 6%.

From January 1, 2027, Medicare pays ASP minus 28% for drugs a hospital bought through 340B. That is 32% less per dose. CMS estimates the cut at $4.65 billion in a year, counting patients’ copays (final rule, p. 1213). The law requires these changes to be budget neutral, so the same $4.65 billion goes back out as a 7.5% raise on everything that isn’t a separately paid drug, at every hospital paid this way (final rule, p. 110, 1213).

Three groups don’t take the drug cut: rural sole community hospitals, children’s hospitals and cancer hospitals (final rule, p. 661). Vaccines and new drugs still on pass-through status are exempt too. Every hospital gets the raise.

A second, smaller cut rides along. From 2018 to 2022 Medicare paid 340B drugs at ASP minus 22.5%, raised everything else to match, and lost in the Supreme Court (American Hospital Association v. Becerra, 2022). It repaid the 340B hospitals about $9 billion, then started taking back the $7.8 billion that everything else had been overpaid (final rule, p. 523). That “remedy offset” was 0.5% a year. In 2027 it becomes 2 points off the payment update, until the money is recovered, which CMS expects in 2031 (final rule, p. 552). It applies to almost every hospital that billed Medicare before 2018, 340B or not.

One number decides most hospitals

A hospital loses 32 cents of each 340B drug dollar and gains 7.5 cents on each of its other dollars. If all its drugs are 340B, those cancel when 340B drugs are 19% of its Medicare outpatient payments. Above that line, the swap costs it money. About 33% of hospitals in CMS’s estimate are 340B hospitals; by this model, 569 of the 1,145 are above the line.

Move the rate to see how the picture shifts. ASP minus 33.4% is what CMS’s 2026 survey of hospitals’ drug costs found, and what CMS proposed in July. It settled on 28%, a figure built from 340B ceiling prices (final rule, p. 630, 634).

Who comes out ahead

CMS’s own estimate, by hospital group, before any modeling here. For-profit hospitals can’t join 340B, so they take no drug cut and keep the whole raise. Large teaching hospitals and those with the most low-income patients lose the most.

The money doesn’t travel as far as it looks. By this model, 53% of the raise goes to 340B hospitals themselves, because they also do a lot of non-drug work. Net, about $2.3 billion leaves today’s 340B hospitals for everyone else, and 580 of 1,145 of them come out behind.

The losses are concentrated. Fifty hospitals carry 45% of all the dollars lost. The gains are spread thin across thousands.

How this was built

What is CMS’s, and what is modeled

Each hospital’s total change is CMS’s: its estimated 2026 and 2027 outpatient payments from the final rule’s hospital impact file, simulated from 2025 claims, Medicare and patient copays together. CMS doesn’t publish how much of each hospital’s total is 340B drugs. So the split is modeled here, and the last line, everything else, is whatever is left of CMS’s total. The parts always add up to CMS’s number.

  • Drug share. From each hospital’s latest cost report (HCRIS, worksheet D part V): Medicare outpatient drug charges as a share of all its Medicare outpatient charges, converted to a payment share with one national factor (1.1) chosen so the model reproduces CMS’s published results for for-profit and rural hospitals, which have no 340B cut.
  • 340B drug share. Two estimates, blended 3 to 1. The first works back from CMS’s total: what 340B share leaves CMS’s number once the raise, the offset and the rule’s typical other changes are counted. The second is the hospital’s own 340B history: what Medicare withheld from it in 2021 under the old cut, from CMS’s 2023 remedy file, grown to today’s volume. All of it is scaled so the national cut equals CMS’s $4.65 billion. Only hospitals in 340B during 2025 get a drug cut.
  • Offset. CMS’s Addendum R lists who pays it. Its size is the ratio of the two 2027 conversion factors, $98.011 to $99.970, applied the way CMS’s estimate applies it: to the hospital’s whole 2027 payment (Table 112 shows −2.0% for every group, p. 1216). In actual claims the offset applies only to non-drug services (Addendum A), so for drug-heavy hospitals the real offset is somewhat smaller than CMS’s estimate implies.
  • Who counts as 340B. A hospital with an HRSA 340B registration in force at any time in 2025, the year of claims CMS simulated. Hospitals that joined in 2026 have no cut in CMS’s numbers, though they will in 2027; their statements say so.

How it was checked

  • CMS publishes the 340B effect for 50 hospital groups (Table 112, column 4). Added up the same way, the model matches them within 0.17 percentage points on average.
  • The modeled raise totals $4.52 billion, 97% of the $4.65 billion cut, without being fitted to it. Budget neutrality says they should match.
  • The modeled offset totals $1.64 billion for 3,240 hospitals, without being fitted. CMS projects about $1.6 billion (p. 1208).
  • What is left over (everything else) is spread about the same for 340B hospitals as for the rest, centered between 2% and 3%, near the 2.3% update.

Limits

  • A single hospital’s split can be off, most of all for hospitals whose two estimates disagree; its statement shows the range. The total is CMS’s and isn’t a forecast of what the hospital will actually be paid.
  • Cost reports are a year or two old, and 340B participation is HRSA’s list on Nov. 5, 2026.
  • Off-campus departments paid under the physician fee schedule also take the 340B cut, without a raise; CMS puts that at $640 million and it isn’t in the hospital file (p. 668).
